# 0033. The relay is a dumb pipe that keeps nothing (replaces 0006)

## Context
A phone has to reach the daemon on a Mac that is often behind a company network, where nothing may listen and only outbound HTTPS gets out. [0006](0006-remote-through-relay.md) proposed a relay; the owner settled the details (`nushi-mobile-research.md`, "Owner decisions"): anyone may run one, the user picks it, and it learns as little as possible.

## Decision
- `nushi-relay` is one static Go binary (`relay/`, `cmd/nushi-relay`). A listener waits at `GET /v1/listen/{id}`, a dialer comes to `GET /v1/dial/{id}`; each is a WebSocket. Once a dialer has sent its first message (the dialer always speaks first), the relay pairs it with the listener waiting on the same id and copies binary messages between them until either side goes. A listener that speaks before it has a dialer is closed; a dialer that finds none within 10 s is closed.
- Only the holder of an id's key listens there. An id is the SHA-256 of a listen key's public half (Ed25519, with a domain tag; the daemon's own, apart from its Noise key). The relay sends a new listener 32 fresh random bytes; it listens only once it answers, within 10 s, the public key and its signature of the bytes and the id. So knowing an id (a revoked phone, anyone who saw a `ws://` URL) is enough to dial it, never to listen. A second listener that proves the key replaces the one waiting: that is the same daemon, back. Phones only dial.
- It knows nothing about who is talking: no accounts, no tokens, no disk, no database. It logs only that it started and fatal errors (Go's HTTP error log, which names client addresses, is discarded). It has no metrics.
- In memory only, caps keep it from being a free bandwidth pipe or something one client can fill: 64 KiB a message, 1 MiB/s a connection after an 8 MiB burst (each message and each ping costs at least 512 bytes), 90 s of silence (pings count), a listener waits an hour at most (it connects again), 16 dialers an id (the id's own listener is never kept out), 1,000 in all. Per client address (an IPv4 address or an IPv6 /64): 200 connections, never more than half of the total, and 2 new ones a second after a burst of 60. Many people can share one company address, so these are generous. Plain connections (not WebSockets, or not yet) count apart: 8 per address, coming at the same rate as WebSockets (the address is the TCP peer's, or behind a proxy the header's once a request has come), and 1,000 in all. New ones that haven't sent a request yet (behind a proxy, whose address isn't known yet) have a budget of 100 and 2 s for their first headers; when the budget or the total is spent, the oldest of them makes way for a new one, else the one idle longest, so a flood of idle or slow sockets can't keep a daemon or a phone out. A later request gets 5 s for its headers, and a connection is closed after 5 s idle between requests. All are flags.
- Those per-address counts are kept under a keyed hash of the address (the key is random each start), never the address, and dropped once the address has no connection and its rate is back. Behind a reverse proxy, `-ip-header` names the header with the client's address; without it the proxy's address is the only one. Of a list header such as `X-Forwarded-For` the relay takes the last entry (or the `-ip-hops`-th from the right, with several proxies appending), never the first: a proxy appends after what the client wrote.
- TLS is optional: built in (`-host`, Let's Encrypt through autocert, the certificate in memory unless `-cert-cache` names a folder), or plain behind a reverse proxy. `ws://` is allowed; the security doesn't rest on it.
- Requests with an `Origin` header (a web page's script) are refused.

## What the relay can still see
- IP addresses of both ends: where the phone is when it checks, and the Mac's (company) egress.
- Rendezvous ids, and the listen keys' public halves: the daemon's long-lived id (one per daemon, so it links every phone connection to that daemon; it changes when a device is revoked) and each one-time pairing id. Ids are random and carry no names. The URL path holds them, so without TLS the networks in between see them too.
- Timing and sizes: when connections open and close, when each message passes and how big it is. That shows when someone works, how long turns run, roughly how much the agent writes, and when an answer comes.
- How many connections a daemon has, and the WebSocket framing.
- The hosting provider (Fly.io, a VPS's host, a TLS-terminating proxy in front) sees the same.

## What it can't
- Anything inside the messages: they are Noise transport messages ([0034](0034-noise-between-phone-and-daemon.md)), encrypted and authenticated end to end. It can't read, change, replay, reorder or inject one without the connection failing.
- Which device connects: a phone's public key travels encrypted inside the handshake.
- The daemon's public key and the pairing secret: they reach the phone through the QR code, never through the relay.

## Consequences
- The relay can drop, delay or refuse traffic (availability is not protected), and can make a daemon do a handshake's work per dial (capped by its own limits and the daemon's).
- Anyone who knows a daemon's id can dial it, which costs the daemon a handshake, and can hold its 16 dialer slots, which keeps its phones out while it does (availability); it can't listen there, so it never gets a phone's dial. Only a paired phone gets past the handshake.
- The proof binds an honest relay. A dishonest one (its operator) can route a dial anywhere; against it only Noise protects, as above.
- Running without `-cert-cache` asks Let's Encrypt for a certificate on every start; frequent restarts hit its rate limits, so a host that restarts often should keep one.

## Amendments
- 2026-10-27: with `-web` and `-origin` the relay also serves the phone's web app, from memory with a strict CSP, and takes a WebSocket from exactly that app's origin; any other `Origin` is still refused (ADR 0054).
- 2026-11-04: listening needs only a key of one's own, so a few addresses could hold every connection for an hour at a time; listeners from one address (an IPv4 address or an IPv6 /48) are now capped at 16 (`-listen-per-ip`, 0 for none), far below the 200 connections. A listener's slot is held until its connection ends, a phone's session on it included, since the daemon waits again as soon as a phone is matched: one Mac holds its waiting listener, one per live phone session (a stale one until Idle, 90 s), one per id it gave up that a phone still needs, and a pairing's, so several Macs behind one NAT with a few phones or tabs each share the 16; past it a daemon's wait gets 503 and backs off. Freeing the slot at the match instead would let one address listen, dial itself and listen again without end, the dialer's connection counting only against the 200. Filling the relay now takes some 63 addresses or /48s, not five; an allowlist of listen ids or keys would close it but breaks with each pairing and revocation, which take a fresh listen key.
- 2026-11-04: `-ip-header` is believed only from a trusted proxy: a loopback peer by default, or else only the addresses `-trusted-proxy` names (which replace loopback rather than add to it). Any other peer is counted under its own TCP address from the accept, so a client that reaches the relay past the proxy can't pick a new address for each request and slip the per-address caps. The listener cap counts that same address, an IPv6 one's /48: a peer that isn't a trusted proxy has all its listeners under its own address whatever header it sends, and behind a proxy the relay doesn't trust every daemon behind it shares the proxy's address and its 16 listeners.
- 2026-11-04: "capped by the daemon's limits" had no cap behind it: a dishonest relay that answered every listen at once with a replayed first message made the daemon hold thousands of half-done handshakes. The daemon now holds at most 16 (`MaxConns`) handshakes not yet past their first transport message: an id that a dialer came to listens again only once one has a slot; a handshake has 10 s (it was 30 s). The waits themselves take no slot, one per id still waited on after a revoke, so ids given up can't leave the daemon's own none.
- 2026-11-05: 16 listeners an address were fewer than one daemon may hold: its own wait, one for each of its 16 sessions (`MaxConns`), one at each of the 8 ids given up it still waits at, and a pairing's, 26 in all; with 8 ids given up, its own id's ninth session never connected. The cap is now 32 (filling the relay with listeners takes some 31 addresses or /48s), and past it (several Macs behind one address) the daemon's own id comes first: the waits at ids given up listen only while its own waits, and give their slots back when its wait fails.
- 2026-11-05: the listener cap left dialers, which need no key, counted per /64 alone: an IPv6 /48 holds 65,536 /64s, each with its own 200 and its own rate, so silent dialers to made-up ids from a few dozen /64s of one /48 filled the 1,000 and every daemon's wait got 503. All WebSockets from one /48 (an IPv4 address counts as its own) are now counted together too, 250 at most (never more than half of the total), so filling the relay takes four /48s or five IPv4 addresses.
- 2026-11-13: a release's relay has the web app built in ([0113](0113-the-relay-has-the-web-app-built-in.md)) and serves it only when `-origin` is given; without it the relay is the pipe above.
- 2026-11-21 (security review of 2026-11-18, finding 1): **slow readers held every plain connection.** A client that asks for a big file and never reads it keeps its connection busy until the file's write deadline (15 s plus a second per 16 KiB: minutes for the app's biggest files), and only fresh or idle connections made way; plain connections were capped per /64 alone, so some 125 /64s of one /48, eight each, held all 1,000, and every new connection was closed at its accept, a daemon's or a phone's WebSocket upgrade included, for as long as they were renewed. A front that reads the answer at full speed and buffers it (cloudflared) likely hid this; `-host`, or any front that doesn't buffer, didn't. Now plain connections from one /48 (an IPv4 address is its own) are counted together, 64 at most (`-plain-per-net`; never under `-plain-per-ip`, never over half the total): a phone loading the app holds about six (a browser's connections to one host), so 64 is the documented `-plain-per-ip 32` of two /64s, or eight /64s at the default 8, and filling the 1,000 takes 16 /48s, or 125 IPv4 addresses as before. And when the 1,000 are open and none is fresh or idle, a connection in the middle of an answer that isn't a WebSocket's makes way: from the /48 holding the most plain connections, the one answering longest. A phone on a poor link, its handful open, goes only once no network holds more than it does (some 160 networks of six each). A WebSocket is never shed: it is hijacked out of the plain count as it upgrades. Over HTTP/2 (`-host` offers it) one connection carries many streams, each with its own deadline, but it counts as one plain connection, under the same caps, and makes way whole. A proxy's pooled connection that carries another client's request than the one before now costs that client none of its rate, only its caps: it isn't a new connection of the client's.
- 2026-11-21 (the same review, finding 2): **at the total, a dialer without a listener makes way.** All 1,000 WebSockets were refused once open, and four /48s of silent dialers to made-up ids, renewed every 10 s, held them. Now the oldest silent dialer makes way for a new connection, else the one waiting longest for a listener; each held its place 20 s at most anyway, so it costs one who means it a dial again. Listeners and paired dialers stay, and with only those open a new connection is refused as before. What one /48 or IPv4 address can still hold without renewing is its 32 listeners (an hour each) and as many dialers paired with them, which pings keep open: 64, so filling the relay takes 16 of them, not four. Closing that too would mean ending a listener's session with its own address's dialer, and a phone at home on the Mac's network is exactly that; it stays a known limit.
